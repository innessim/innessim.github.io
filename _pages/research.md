Speciation is one of the fundamental processes generating biodiversity, yet relatively little is known about how this process occurs, particularly in its early stages. My research examines how populations diverge and become reproductively isolated during the early stages of speciation. I am particularly interested in how environmental variation and demographic history shape divergence among populations, and how these processes can, in turn, influence the evolution of reproductive isolation. I combine field and common-garden experiments, range-wide crossing designs, phenotypic analyses, and population genomics to connect ecological variation with the evolutionary processes that drive speciation.

## Evolution and the origins of reproductive isolation

### How do environmental differences shape population divergence?

<strong class="research-result">Repeated trait evolution along environmental gradients</strong><br>
Environmental heterogeneity can promote different combinations of traits, but it remains unclear whether populations exposed to similar climates repeatedly evolve similar phenotypes or whether these patterns are instead shaped by demographic history. In *Mimulus moschatus*, I found that suites of traits repeatedly track climatic gradients despite differences in evolutionary history. Populations from longer growing seasons tended to delay flowering and invest more in growth, whereas those from shorter growing seasons tended to reproduce earlier and invest less in long-term growth, despite differences in demographic history and patterns of gene flow among populations. These patterns suggest that similar environmental conditions can drive repeated phenotypic evolution across genetically divergent clusters.

Phytochemical variation provides another axis along which populations may diverge. Environmental heterogeneity can generate geographic variation in plant defenses, potentially contributing to divergence among populations. In *M. moschatus*, total PPG concentrations and chemical profiles varied among populations, [with patterns closely paralleling those found in the related *M. guttatus*](/files/Innes_2025.pdf). This similarity suggests that shared environmental pressures may lead to repeated evolutionary responses in chemical defenses.

<strong class="research-result">Continued adaptation to climatic gradients</strong><br>
Climate varies across both space and time, but how populations continue to evolve along spatial climatic gradients and respond to ongoing climate change remains unclear. Our work in *Trifolium repens* examined whether climate-associated clines in cyanogenesis have shifted over six decades in its native and introduced ranges. Although we found no clear evidence that warming has consistently shifted these clines through time, introduced populations showed continued evolution along novel temperature gradients, with stronger contemporary clines approaching those found in the native range.

<strong class="research-result">Rapid adaptation across climatic gradients</strong><br>
Populations can encounter novel climates both through geographic range shifts and through climate change, but how quickly they adapt to these new conditions—and whether that adaptation keeps pace with environmental change—remains unclear. In a transcontinental common-garden experiment with *Trifolium repens*, I contributed to research examining adaptation to climatic variation across native and introduced ranges. We found evidence of rapid adaptation across latitudinal gradients in the introduced range, but also an adaptation cost when introduced populations were transplanted back into the native range and an adaptation lag in the face of recent warming. These results suggest that populations can evolve rapidly in response to novel climates while still failing to keep pace with ongoing climate change.
