Speciation is one of the fundamental processes generating biodiversity, yet relatively little is known about how this process occurs, particularly in its early stages. My research examines how populations diverge and become reproductively isolated during the early stages of speciation. I am particularly interested in how environmental variation and demographic history shape divergence among populations, and how these processes can, in turn, influence the evolution of reproductive isolation. I combine field and common-garden experiments, range-wide crossing designs, phenotypic analyses, and population genomics to connect ecological variation with the evolutionary processes that drive speciation.

## Evolution and the origins of reproductive isolation

### How do environmental differences and demographic history shape population divergence?

<strong class="research-result"> Demographic history shapes divergence more than geography or climate </strong><br>
Untangling the roles of adaptation and demographic history in driving divergence among populations is central to understanding how reproductive barriers arise. In *Mimulus moschatus*, we found that geographic distance was not a simple predictor of gene flow. Populations in the south and central Sierra Nevada were strongly differentiated from one another despite being geographically close, whereas northern populations showed little differentiation from southern Sierra Nevada populations despite being more than 500 km apart and experiencing substantial climatic differences. These patterns were more consistent with historical demographic processes, such as isolation and expansion following past glaciation, than with contemporary geography or climate restricting gene flow.

<strong class="research-result"> Repeated trait evolution along environmental gradients </strong><br>
Environmental heterogeneity can promote different combinations of traits, but it remains unclear whether populations exposed to similar climates repeatedly evolve similar phenotypes or whether these patterns are instead shaped by demographic history. In *M. moschatus*, I found that suites of traits repeatedly track climatic gradients despite differences in evolutionary history. Populations from longer growing seasons environments tended to delay flowering and invest more in growth, whereas those experiencing shorter growing seasons tended to reproduce earlier and invest less in long-term growth, despite differences in demographic history and patterns of gene flow among populations. These patterns suggest that similar environmental conditions can drive repeated phenotypic evolution across genetically divergent clusters.

Phytochemical variation provides another axis along which populations may diverge. Environmental heterogeneity can generate geographic variation in plant defenses, potentially contributing to divergence among populations. In *M. moschatus*, total concentrations of phenylpropanoid glycosides and chemical profiles varied among populations, [with patterns closely paralleling those found in the related *M. guttatus*](/files/Innes_2025.pdf). This similarity suggests that shared environmental pressures may lead to repeated evolutionary responses in chemical defenses.

<strong class="research-result"> Continued adaptation to climatic gradients </strong><br>
Climate varies across both space and time, but how populations continue to evolve along spatial climatic gradients and respond to ongoing climate change remains unclear. Our work in *Trifolium repens* examined whether climate-associated clines in cyanogenesis have shifted over six decades in its native and introduced ranges. Although we found no clear evidence that warming has consistently shifted these clines through time, introduced populations showed continued evolution along novel temperature gradients, with stronger contemporary clines approaching those found in the native range.

<strong class="research-result"> Rapid adaptation across climatic gradients </strong><br>
Populations can encounter novel climates both through geographic range shifts and through climate change, but how quickly they adapt to these new conditions—and whether that adaptation keeps pace with environmental change—remains unclear. In a transcontinental common-garden experiment with *T. repens*, we examined how adaptation to climate varies across native and introduced ranges. We found evidence of rapid adaptation across latitudinal gradients in the introduced range, but also an adaptation cost when introduced populations were transplanted back into the native range and an adaptation lag in the face of recent warming. These results suggest that populations can evolve rapidly in response to novel climates while still failing to keep pace with ongoing climate change.


### How rapidly can populations evolve in response to environmental change?

<strong class="research-result"> Large structural variants underlie rapid, parallel adaptation </strong><br>
The role of rapid adaptation during invasions has historically been downplayed, since introductions were assumed to involve few colonists with little genetic diversity. Leveraging a worldwide population genomic dataset of *T. repens*, we identified five large haploblocks, with signatures of structural rearrangements such as inversions, that contribute to climate adaptation. These variants were already present as standing genetic variation in the native range, and they form parallel climate-associated clines across introduced ranges on different continents. These results suggest that large-effect structural variants contribute substantially to rapid, parallel adaptation during invasion.

<strong class="research-result"> Responses to extreme climatic events </strong><br>
Extreme climatic events such as heat waves are expected to become more frequent, but how they affect fitness and mating systems in natural populations remains unclear. In a multi-year study of wild *M. guttatus* populations, natural heat waves decreased fitness and altered maternal provisioning. We also found a trend toward increased inbreeding in some of the highest-elevation populations, suggesting that mating system changes may themselves be occurring in response to climate change.


### What predicts reproductive compatibility between populations?

<strong class="research-result"> Nearby populations are less compatible than distant ones </strong><br>
Compatibility between populations is generally expected to decline as they diverge, but it remains unclear how this plays out among populations that can still freely exchange genes. Using a range-wide crossing design in *M. moschatus*, I tested whether geographic and climatic distance predict compatibility between populations. Counterintuitively, compatibility increased with distance, such that populations that were geographically close and climatically similar produced fewer viable seeds than those that were more distant and dissimilar. Variation among crosses was substantial, and no population or parental line was consistently associated with higher or lower seed production. This idiosyncratic pattern suggests that some populations may have recently shifted in mating system, such as toward increased self-fertilization, causing inbreeding depression when crossed with nearby, genetically similar populations.
