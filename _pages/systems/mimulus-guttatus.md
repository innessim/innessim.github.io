*Mimulus guttatus* is one of the most well-studied wildflowers in ecological genetics — a long-running model system I've been fortunate to work within.

## Habitat & range

*Mimulus guttatus* is native to western North America, found from Mexico to Alaska, and it occurs in a wide range of habitats such as coastal bluffs, high alpine seeps, roadside ditches, even geothermal stream banks. Inland annual populations commonly occupy ephemeral streams, seeps, meadows and rock walls, sites that start off very wet but dry out quickly as summer progresses. That fast dry-down drives a short growing season in many populations, often just 40 to 80 days from germination to seed. Not all populations are annual, though. Along the immediate Pacific coast, from southern California up into southern Washington, M. guttatus forms a distinct coastal perennial ecotype. These coastal plants live in habitats that stay damp and foggy through the summer, so unlike their inland annual relatives, they don't face the seasonal drought that forces a fast, one-shot life cycle. Perennial populations also occur further inland, especially at higher elevations in the Sierra Nevada and Cascades, wherever soils hold onto moisture through the summer.

## Natural history

*Mimulus guttatus*' wide range of habitats has allowed it to adapt in a variety of ways, and the result is a species that's remarkably diverse from population to population. Some populations have evolved tolerance to grow on copper mine tailings or serpentine outcrops, soils that would be toxic to most other plants. Others have settled in at high elevation as long-lived perennials, while still others have evolved into fast, self-pollinating lineages with a much shorter life cycle than their progenitors. Together, this patchwork of locally adapted populations makes up what's known as the M. guttatus species complex, and it is part of why the species has become such a productive system for studying how populations adapt and diverge.

## Why I work with it

Few wildflowers pack as much biological range into one species as *M. guttatus* – populations have evolved tolerance to toxic mine tailings, fast self-fertilization life cycles, or long-lived perennial life histories. That, combined with a well-developed set of genomic resources for a wild, non-model system, facilitates what you see in a population out in the field to what's actually happening in its genome. 
Part of why I work with *M. guttatus* also comes down to community. It is a genuinely collaborative system, with a real culture of labs building on each other's work and decades of accumulated resources and knowledge to draw on. I've been able to contribute to a multi-year study of how natural heatwave events affect fitness and maternal provisioning in wild populations, work that has revealed a trend toward increased inbreeding in some of the highest-elevation populations, hinting that mating-system shifts may themselves be a response to a warming climate.
