White clover is easy to overlook — it's the stuff growing in lawns and roadsides nearly everywhere — but that ubiquity is exactly what makes it interesting to study.

## Habitat & range

*Trifolium repens* is native to Europe and western Asia. It has since been introduced essentially everywhere temperate agriculture has gone, and now grows across six continents, in climates its ancestors never experienced. That global spread, over a relatively short evolutionary timescale, makes it a natural experiment in rapid adaptation.

## Natural history

White clover is a low-growing perennial legume, spreading by stolons — horizontal stems that root at the nodes as they creep along the ground, letting a single plant form an expanding mat over time. Its trifoliate leaves, the source of its genus name, usually carry a pale, crescent-shaped chevron near the base of each leaflet. The flowers aren't self-compatible, so clover depends on insects, mainly bees, to cross-pollinate its clustered white or pale pink flower heads. Like other legumes, it fixes nitrogen through a symbiosis with rhizobia living in its roots, and it's known for its cyanogenesis polymorphism — some plants produce a chemical defense against herbivores, others don't, and the frequencies of this trait shift geographically with climate.

## Why I work with it

White clover was my first system, the species I used for my master's research. I examined how frequencies of cyanogenesis — a chemical defense where damaged tissue releases hydrogen cyanide — have shifted over a 60-year period, in both clover's native European range and its introduced ranges. Cyanogenic plants are generally favoured in warmer climates, where the defense's benefits outweigh its costs, while non-cyanogenic plants dominate colder regions, where those same costs outweigh the benefit. Tracking how that balance has shifted over decades, and whether recently introduced populations show the same pattern as those in white clover's native range, gave me an early, direct look at just how fast a widespread species can respond to novel climates. <br>

I've also contributed to the [GLobal Urban Evolution Project (GLUE)](https://www.globalurbanevolution.com/), my actual first taste of research — a study sampling over 110,000 clover plants across 160 cities worldwide, which found that this same chemical defense evolved along urban-rural gradients in nearly half of those cities. In most cases the defense was more common further from the urban core, likely tied to lower drought stress there, but the direction and strength of that pattern varied city to city. More recently, I've co-authored a transcontinental common-garden study testing how clover has adapted to climate across its native European and introduced North American ranges. I've also co-authored a related study identifying the specific structural genomic variants underlying that adaptation — variants that rapidly increased in frequency following its global introduction.
